## Population vs sample

- **Population** (size $N$): everyone of interest. Example: all **100,000** people in city XYZ.
- **Sample** (size $n$): the part we actually measure. Example: **100** people chosen from the city.

Weighing all 100,000 residents is impractical, so we weigh a sample of 100 and use its average to **estimate** the average weight of the whole city.

---

## Formulas

**Sample mean** (a **statistic**):

$$\bar x = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i}{n} = \frac{x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n}{n}$$

**Population mean** (a **parameter**):

$$\mu = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i}{N} = \frac{x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_N}{N}$$

The formulas look the same. The only difference is **lowercase $n$** (sample size) versus **capital $N$** (population size).

| | Symbol | Size | Called a |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| Sample mean | $\bar x$ | $n$ | **statistic** |
| Population mean | $\mu$ | $N$ | **parameter** |

---

## Bigger samples give better estimates

We want $\bar x$ to be as close as possible to $\mu$. **Increasing the sample size** makes $\bar x$ a better estimate of $\mu$. Going from $n = 100$ to $n = 1{,}000$ gives a more accurate estimate, but it is ten times as much work.

> **Trade-off:** more accuracy requires more data, and more data requires more effort.

---

## Key takeaways

- $\bar x$ (sample, $n$) is a **statistic**. $\mu$ (population, $N$) is a **parameter**.
- Both are "sum ÷ count". Only the group being averaged differs.
- Larger samples make $\bar x$ a more reliable estimate of $\mu$.
